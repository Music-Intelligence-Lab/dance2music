Song versions (Duet, Song): the upper line goes out on channel 1, the lower on channel 3, each to one monophonic instrument (a SWAM solo string is made for this). Loudness on CC 11, vibrato depth on CC 1 and rate on CC 19, brightness on CC 74. As slurred notes (the default) it is right on any instrument, which slides between the notes by itself. As one note a bow with pitch bend every glide is exactly the page's, but the instrument's bend range must be set to 24 semitones up and down first, or the line barely leaves its first note. A solo instrument suits a sung line better than a section.

Each sustained voice carries its own dynamic on CC 11, vibrato growing into held notes on CC 1, tremolo on CC 21, brightness on CC 74.

Versions. The menu at the top holds named versions of the mapping. The row of scores under the timeline is a judge that reads the notes (voice leading, melody, harmony, fit to her movement, expression); it ranks versions and says why, but it cannot hear. Trust your ears over it.

Song (the Duet and Song versions in the menu at the top). No harmony: one or two sung lines, and the body shapes how they are played.

  • Hand height → pitch. Each hand's height, from her hip to above her head, is a place on a scale. A slow hand steps from note to note. A fast hand does not run the scale: the note is held while the hand flies and the line leaps to where it lands, sliding into the note (longer for a wider leap).
  • Open hand → bow. An open hand is the bow on the string. A fist lifts it: silence. A hand hanging at her side, or still for a few seconds, also rests its line, so a phrase is one excursion of the hand. The hands are read in the page by a second model, loaded when a Song version is first chosen (one hand every third frame); a hand it cannot read counts as open, so from far away only hanging and still hands rest a line.
  • Hand speed → loudness. The hand's speed is the bow's speed, on top of a slow phrase level from the whole body. A rising line swells a little; a sudden movement accents; a new stroke on the same note is a new bow.
  • Stillness → vibrato. Vibrato grows into every held note and stops while the line moves.
  • Reach → colour. A hand far from the body is bright, a hand held close is dark; a body low to the floor darkens both.
  • Two lines. Her right hand is the upper line, her left the lower, a tenth below at the same height. A leap lands on a consonance with the other line, and a dissonance that is held melts by step after a moment.

Octave texture. Four real voices: the bass walks the scale with her height (Rule of the Octave), the hands lead the top line, violas and cellos fill the chord. Chord-map texture (Nov 14) is described below.

The body arranges. Three things it does, three jobs in the music:

  • Shape → harmony. Where her weight and axis are picks the chord on the map under the video. A new shape has to hold for a moment; the chord then changes on her next landing (a stroke ending, an accent, a foot coming down). Coming home from the dark chords passes through V.
  • Shape → voicing. How much space she takes spreads the five sections from a close huddle to four octaves and brings the basses in; hands reaching up lift the violins. Open shapes add 9ths and 7ths.
  • Limbs → lines. Decisive arm strokes are notes in the first violins: the stroke's rise or fall is the interval, slow strokes land on chord tones. Leg gestures in the air sing in the cellos; steps pluck the basses; sudden hands pluck the violas; hand circles roll the harp and a half turn sweeps it.
  • Effort → sound. How much she has been moving is the dynamic (soft bow to full bow, darker to brighter). Sudden, bound movement turns the bow to tremolo. The range is kept moderate (about 9 dB) and a swell falls slowly (5 s) when the movement stops, so the piece keeps one level; stillness is a little softer, and only a long one (15 s) fades to nothing.
